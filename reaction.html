Reaction Wheel Attitude Control

Independent personal project • Completed August 2026

A single-axis attitude-control testbed developed from first-principles rigid-body dynamics through a finished closed-loop hardware demonstration. I modeled the angular-momentum exchange, designed and fabricated the rotating platform and reaction-wheel assembly, integrated sensing, power, motor drive, and Bluetooth communication, and developed feedback control that repeatedly commanded the body to approximately ±30°.

Scope End-to-end personal project

Dynamics, CAD, fabrication, electronics, embedded control, communications, commissioning, and validation.

1.26° final error in a closed-loop target test, with comparable tracking at 25°.

Control stack AS5600 + IMU + Arduino

Status Complete & demonstrated

Wireless command, live telemetry, bidirectional closed-loop attitude changes, and a recorded final demo.

Final Demonstration

Closing the loop on the physical system

The finished controller accepts attitude commands wirelessly from a desktop, measures body angle with an AS5600 magnetic encoder, uses gyroscope rate feedback for damping, and drives the internal reaction wheel until the platform approaches the commanded orientation.

In final testing, a 30° target settled at 28.74° and a 25° target settled at 23.55°. The recorded demo shows the physical platform responding while measured angle and commanded target are plotted live on the monitor behind it.

Core physical relationship
τbody ≈ −Iwheel αwheel

Body torque comes from changing the wheel's angular momentum. This also explains the system's eventual momentum-saturation limit: once the wheel can no longer meaningfully accelerate, additional steady wheel speed produces little additional body torque.

Mechanical Design

Designing for reaction torque, low body inertia, and serviceability

The platform rotates about a central shaft and bearing support while the reaction wheel is driven internally by a geared DC motor. I used a bolt-weighted wheel to increase rotational inertia and enclosed it with a removable printed guard for normal operation.

Battery placement became a meaningful design variable because the batteries made up a large fraction of the moving body's mass. An early layout concentrated cells near the platform edge; I replaced it with three two-cell holders distributed closer to the rotation axis to reduce body moment of inertia and improve the wheel-to-body authority.

The final structure remained intentionally modular: the wheel guard removes for inspection, electronics stay accessible from above, and the central bearing/shaft assembly can be reached independently of the control hardware.

Electronics & Control

Measured angle, rate damping, and wireless commanding

An AS5600 magnetic encoder provides absolute platform angle while the IMU supplies angular-rate information. The controller combines attitude error with rate damping, then maps the result into signed motor commands with minimum-drive compensation, output limiting, and slew-rate limiting.

A SparkFun BlueSMiRF v2 provides the final wireless serial link on the Nano's D9/D10 software UART. Commands and telemetry run at 9600 baud, which proved reliable after isolating the Bluetooth and UART sides independently during commissioning.

Simulation Foundation

Starting with the rigid-body dynamics

The project began as an Analytical Dynamics study using Euler's equations, inertia tensors, and conservation of angular momentum. I used Python and MATLAB to explore how wheel torque, wheel speed, initial body rotation, and inertia distribution affect the resulting body motion before committing to the hardware architecture.

These animations are simulation visualizations from that work rather than experimental data. I kept them in the final project because they show the physical model that guided the later testbed.

Commissioning & Iteration

The engineering work was in the integration

Once the first hardware existed, most progress came from isolating one subsystem at a time, measuring what it was actually doing, and changing the design based on that evidence. A few examples capture how the project evolved.

01

Reduced body inertia

Reworked the battery layout from mass concentrated near the perimeter to three two-cell packs arranged closer to the axis. Because the batteries dominated the body mass, this directly improved the mechanical leverage available from the same reaction wheel.

02

Debugged the wireless path by isolation

Verified the Bluetooth radio with a direct TX/RX loopback, then measured the signal arriving at the Nano from repeated serial zeroes. A ~520 μs five-bit low pulse identified the module's UART rate as approximately 9600 baud. The full desktop → Bluetooth → Arduino → Bluetooth → desktop path then passed clean text bidirectionally.

03

Identified reaction-wheel saturation

Large closed-loop commands initially moved the platform and then stalled while the wheel continued spinning. That behavior matched the physics: once wheel angular acceleration approached zero, the wheel was storing momentum but no longer producing meaningful additional body torque. I used that result to define a realistic final control envelope rather than tuning around a physical limit.

Performance

Characterizing what the hardware could actually do

Early open-loop tests established that the platform had substantial transient rotational authority, including roughly a full revolution in high-energy pulse testing. Closed-loop testing exposed the more important practical constraint: a single reaction wheel has finite momentum storage, so large sustained attitude changes eventually saturate the actuator.

I therefore treated approximately ±30° as the useful closed-loop demonstration envelope for this hardware configuration. Within that range, the system produced repeatable, visually clear attitude changes with degree-scale final error.

Possible Extensions

Where I would take the system next

The present testbed is intentionally simple: one controlled rotational axis, a single reaction wheel, and no dedicated momentum-unloading mechanism. That makes the current ±30° closed-loop envelope useful for demonstrating the core dynamics while also making the next design questions fairly clear.

01

Momentum management

Add direct wheel-speed measurement and a momentum-management strategy so the controller can distinguish attitude error from stored wheel momentum and recover authority before the actuator saturates.

02

Higher-fidelity control

Use logged step-response data for formal system identification, then compare the current proportional/rate-damped controller with state-space or LQR control using a measured model of the body, wheel, motor, and friction.

03

Multi-axis architecture

Extend the mechanical design toward two or three orthogonal wheels, move the electronics onto a compact custom PCB, and use the resulting platform to explore coupled spacecraft attitude dynamics rather than a single-axis approximation.

Engineering Ownership

Carrying an open-ended technical goal to a finished result

This was my first substantial personal engineering project, and unlike a class project it came without a supplied architecture, build procedure, or definition of done. I owned the complete path from the initial dynamics model through CAD, fabrication, electronics, embedded software, communications, troubleshooting, controls, and final validation.

The most important outcome is not any single component. It is the integrated system: a first-principles idea translated into hardware, debugged across mechanical, electrical, and software boundaries, characterized against its physical limits, and carried through to a measured demonstration. That end-to-end execution is the standard I want to bring to future engineering work.
