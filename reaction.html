Reaction Wheel Attitude Control

Independent personal project • Completed August 2026

I built a single-axis reaction-wheel testbed, starting with a rigid-body dynamics model and eventually getting closed-loop control working on the physical hardware. I designed the rotating platform and wheel, assembled the electronics, and wrote the control and wireless communication code. The final setup could command attitude changes of roughly ±30°.

Scope Independent build

Simulation, CAD, 3D printing, electronics, embedded code, and testing.

Measured result 30° command → 28.74°

1.26° final error in a closed-loop target test, with comparable tracking at 25°.

Control stack AS5600 + IMU + Arduino

Absolute angle and angular-rate feedback drive a DC-motor reaction wheel through onboard control electronics.

Status Tested and documented

Wireless command, live telemetry, bidirectional closed-loop attitude changes, and a recorded final demo.

Final Demonstration

The final test

The desktop sends a target angle over Bluetooth. An AS5600 encoder measures the platform angle, an IMU supplies angular-rate feedback, and the Arduino adjusts the wheel motor to bring the body toward the target.

A 30° command settled at 28.74°, and a 25° command settled at 23.55°. The video shows the platform moving while the measured and commanded angles are plotted behind it.

Core physical relationship
τbody ≈ −Iwheel αwheel

Body torque comes from changing the wheel's angular momentum. This also explains the system's eventual momentum-saturation limit: once the wheel can no longer meaningfully accelerate, additional steady wheel speed produces little additional body torque.

Mechanical Design

Designing the platform

The body rotates on a central shaft and bearing, with a geared DC motor spinning the weighted reaction wheel inside it. I added bolts to the wheel for more inertia and a removable 3D-printed guard around it.

I kept the guard removable, the electronics accessible from above, and the bearing assembly separate so I could keep making changes without tearing everything apart.

Electronics & Control

Sensors and wireless control

The AS5600 measures absolute angle, and the IMU gives angular rate for damping. I mapped the controller output to signed motor commands, with minimum drive, output limits, and slew-rate limiting.

A SparkFun BlueSMiRF v2 provides the final wireless serial link on the Nano's D9/D10 software UART. Commands and telemetry run at 9600 baud, which proved reliable after isolating the Bluetooth and UART sides independently during commissioning.

Early simulations

Starting with the dynamics

This started as an Analytical Dynamics study of Euler’s equations, inertia tensors, and angular momentum. I wrote Python and MATLAB simulations to see how wheel torque, wheel speed, and inertia distribution affected body motion before building anything.

Commissioning & Iteration

Problems I ran into

A lot of the build came down to testing parts separately and figuring out what was actually going wrong. Three problems took most of my attention:

01

Moving the batteries inward

The original battery layout put too much mass near the edge. I moved the cells inward into three two-cell packs to lower the body’s moment of inertia.

02

Getting Bluetooth working

I tested the Bluetooth module with a TX/RX loopback, then measured the serial waveform reaching the Nano. A roughly 520 μs low pulse across five zero bits pointed to 9600 baud. After fixing that, the desktop and Arduino could exchange commands and telemetry in both directions.

03

Hitting the wheel limit

On larger commands, the body would begin turning and then stall even as the wheel kept spinning. Once wheel acceleration fell toward zero, it was no longer supplying much torque. That was a real momentum-storage limit, not something I could fix by tuning the controller.

Performance

Testing and limits

In early open-loop pulse tests, the body could rotate roughly a full turn. Closed-loop tests were more limited: without a way to unload wheel momentum, the actuator eventually saturates on larger sustained moves.

I settled on about ±30° as the practical closed-loop range for this version. Within that range, I could repeatedly command changes with degree-scale final error.

Possible Extensions

Next steps

This version has one controlled axis and no momentum unloading. If I return to it, these are the main changes I would make.

01

Momentum management

Add direct wheel-speed measurement and a momentum-management strategy so the controller can distinguish attitude error from stored wheel momentum and recover authority before the actuator saturates.

02

Higher-fidelity control

Use logged step-response data for formal system identification, then compare the current proportional/rate-damped controller with state-space or LQR control using a measured model of the body, wheel, motor, and friction.

03

Multi-axis architecture

Extend the mechanical design toward two or three orthogonal wheels, move the electronics onto a compact custom PCB, and use the resulting platform to explore coupled spacecraft attitude dynamics rather than a single-axis approximation.
