Biomimetic Robotics

Nauplius-Inspired Swimming Robot

October 2024 – January 2026

I worked with Nathan Brewer and Professor Carr Everbach on a robot based on the swimming stroke of a barnacle nauplius. We built a single-motor mechanism to drive three pairs of limbs, then packaged it with waterproof electronics and a light-activated control system.

RoleMechanical design & integration

Mechanism development, CAD, prototyping, fabrication, enclosure design, and testing.

SystemThree-pair swimming mechanism

A single motor drives coordinated periodic strokes through a geared linkage system.

MilestoneWorking prototype

Demonstrated phase-controlled limb motion, waterproof packaging, and light activation.

ToolsFusion 360 + fabrication

CAD, mechanism design, 3D printing, electronics packaging, assembly, and iteration.

Motivation

Why a nauplius?

The barnacle nauplius uses an asymmetric, phased swimming stroke to move through a highly viscous environment near the Stokes-flow regime. At that scale, inertia contributes very little, so the order and timing of each limb motion strongly affect whether the organism produces net propulsion.

In collaboration with researchers at the University of Washington School of Oceanography, our goal was to create a physical model that reproduced the organism's appendage motion closely enough to study its swimming dynamics under controlled conditions.

Mechanism

One motor, six coordinated limbs

One motor drives all six limbs through a gear train and linkages. The main challenge was matching both the angular stroke of each limb and the timing between the three pairs.

We adjusted pivot positions, linkage lengths, and gear timing together, while keeping the mechanism small enough to resemble the nauplius’s proportions.

Interactive model of the core transmission and linkage system.

Mechanical Validation

First working mechanism

This was the first version that moved all three limb pairs together. The motor could run continuously while the strokes stayed in phase despite real assembly tolerances.

Mechanism Detail

Inside the mechanism

The stacked gears, supports, and limb pivots had to fit into a small space without becoming impossible to assemble or repair. These photos show how the motor’s motion reaches the limbs.

Build & Integration

Putting the robot together

Once the mechanism worked, we still had to fit the electronics inside a waterproof housing and keep the outside shape close enough to the nauplius for fluid tests.

The finished prototype brought the transmission, supports, appendages, and control electronics into one assembly. We went through several builds to sort out problems that were not obvious when the parts were tested separately.

Control System

Starting it with light

Direct access to the robot is limited during viscous-fluid testing, so the control system uses a light sensor as a simple non-invasive trigger. Applying a flashlight activates the motor; removing the light stops it.

Project status

My part of the project ended in January 2026. Other students at Swarthmore have continued developing it.
